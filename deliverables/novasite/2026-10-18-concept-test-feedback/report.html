2. Executive Summary

This study evaluated Nova Site's commercial positioning across a stratified cohort of 20 South African SME decision-makers paired with live regional price-anchor telemetry; findings represent directional econometric hypotheses rather than guaranteed sales forecasts. Purchase intent is very low (mean 2.2, top-2 5%), with 85% of decision-twins scoring 2 out of 5, and no segment exceeds 17% top-2 intent. Appeal is moderate (mean 2.85), driven by direct founder contact (85% of respondents), the free first year (60%), and fast delivery claims (55%). However, 60% cite unclear scope and terms, 45% doubt the 1–2 day delivery claim, 45% say the portfolio lacks proof, and 40% see the free-to-paid model as a trap. The Van Westendorp optimal price point is R3,021.75, but dispersion is wide (too cheap median R1,500, too expensive median R18,000), indicating high pricing uncertainty. The independent methodological audit rates confidence as low due to cohort size, potential response-surface leniency, and unverified client-supplied baselines. We recommend ITERATE: resolve trust, domain-ownership, and scope-clarity barriers, then validate in a controlled field sprint.

11. Answers to Your Questions

For a defined booking system or business dashboard, would you choose an upfront project fee or a monthly build, hosting and support plan, and why? Specify required scope, acceptable terms and ZAR price expectations separately for each option.

Synthetic personas overwhelmingly prefer an upfront project fee (18 of 20) to own the asset and avoid open-ended subscriptions. For a booking system, acceptable upfront fees range from R5,000 to R25,000 for simple systems, and R25,000 to R60,000 for complex dashboards. Monthly plans are only considered if under R500–R1,000/month with clear exit terms and data ownership. Required scope includes clear deliverables, a warranty period, and defined support hours.

After a free website and first year of hosting, SSL and maintenance, what would justify paying R1,000/month? What inclusions, lower monthly price or annual payment option would change your decision?

Most personas say R1,000/month is too high for basic hosting and maintenance. To justify it, inclusions must cover security updates, backups, uptime monitoring, a few content edits, and quick support. A lower monthly fee of R500–R700 or a discounted annual payment (e.g., R5,000–R9,000/year) would make it more acceptable. Without these, many would walk before the free year ends.

Would you choose a free website with later recurring fees or a low-cost paid website package with transparent ongoing costs? Explain trust concerns, budget and conditions for committing.

The majority (17 of 20) prefer a low-cost paid package with transparent ongoing costs. Free-then-fee models trigger distrust, fear of hidden fees, and concerns about domain or hosting lock-in. Conditions for committing include clear ownership of domain and content, written cancellation terms, and predictable monthly costs (e.g., R300–R500/month).

What do you currently spend on websites, booking tools and administration, who approves the purchase, and what specific event would prompt you to switch?

Current spend varies: R150–R1,200/month on hosting and tools, plus R5,000–R50,000 every few years on website builds. Most owners approve purchases themselves, sometimes with a spouse for larger amounts. Switching triggers include a costly double-booking, lost enquiry, unreliable current supplier, or a clear demonstration of time savings (e.g., 3–5 hours/week).

Which single service would you buy first, what measurable outcome would justify its cost, and what evidence would you need before paying?

Most would buy a booking system or custom dashboard first, expecting reduced double-bookings and admin time savings (e.g., 3–5 hours/week). Measurable outcomes include fewer missed appointments and faster client response. Evidence required: a live demo, contactable references from similar businesses, written scope with fixed price, and a trial or money-back period.

13. Action Plan

1. What should I do next?

Run a structured real-customer validation sprint focused on the two most promising offers: (1) a paid website entry package with transparent ongoing costs, and (2) a fixed-fee custom booking system or dashboard with optional support. Test these against the free website offer to measure conversion and willingness to pay.

2. Why this option?

Synthetic data shows low intent for the current free-then-R1,000/month model, but moderate appeal for the underlying services. Real customers are needed to validate whether the barriers are messaging or fundamental. Alternatives like a pure free offer or a high monthly subscription are not supported by the data.

3. What should I change?

Replace the free website offer with a low-cost paid entry package (e.g., R2,500–R5,000 once-off) that includes clear ownership, cancellation terms, and transparent ongoing hosting/support (e.g., R300–R500/month). For custom apps, offer a fixed project fee (e.g., R15,000–R40,000 depending on scope) with an optional support retainer (e.g., R800–R1,500/month). Publish real client references and live sites on client domains.

4. How do I implement it?

Create two landing pages: one for the paid website package and one for the custom booking system. Drive traffic via LinkedIn outreach (50–100 contacts/month) and Google Business Profile. Use interview questions from the persona answers to qualify leads: 'What do you currently spend on websites and admin?', 'What would make you switch?', 'What evidence do you need before paying?' Offer a paid pilot for the booking system at a reduced fixed fee in exchange for a testimonial and reference.

5. How will I know it worked?

Track: (1) landing page conversion to enquiry, (2) qualified leads per 100 outreach contacts, (3) conversion to paid pilot, (4) actual price accepted vs. proposed, (5) post-pilot willingness to pay for ongoing support. Decision criteria: if fewer than 5% of qualified leads convert to paid within 60 days, revisit the offer or targeting.

6. What happens afterward?

After 60 days, review conversion data and customer feedback. If paid website package converts at >5% and booking system pilot converts at >10%, proceed to scale with refined pricing. If not, iterate on messaging, pricing, or target segment, or consider a narrower focus on custom workflow buyers.

14. Risks and Unknowns

  • Small synthetic sample (n=20) and subgroup instability: segment-level findings are directional only and should not be used for major decisions without larger real-customer validation.
  • AI positivity bias may inflate appeal; purchase intent is the more reliable indicator and is very low.
  • Unverified client claims: Nova Site's project counts, delivery times, reviews, and competitor benchmarks are owner-reported and not independently verified.
  • Free offer may attract non-serious or out-of-scope enquiries, leading to low conversion and wasted resources.
  • Pricing uncertainty: Van Westendorp results show wide dispersion, so the optimal price point (R3,021.75) is a rough guide only.
  • International demand may differ materially from South African pricing expectations and should be assessed separately.
  • Delivery and support costs are unspecified, so gross margin and viability of any price point are unknown.

15. Market Context

South African SMEs in Gauteng appear to buy websites and booking or workflow tools mainly when they need more enquiries, a more credible online presence, and less manual admin, but they are highly price-sensitive for basic sites and much more price-aware for custom systems. Search results from South African pricing guides suggest buyers compare all-in cost across build, hosting, maintenance, and support rather than just launch price, and that custom booking systems or web apps sit far above brochure-site budgets. The free-build offer is likely to attract interest, but the real test is whether buyers accept the post-year R1,000/month website plan or prefer a cheaper monthly, annual, or one-off alternative. Status quo options like WhatsApp, spreadsheets, paper diaries, and Facebook pages remain strong because they are familiar and cost little upfront, even when they create missed enquiries and admin friction.

Trends: Buyers often start from a business pain point rather than a tech preference: missed enquiries, slow follow-up, and manual scheduling are common reasons to look for a website or booking system.; Price comparisons in the market are usually framed as total cost of ownership, not only the initial build price, because hosting, maintenance, updates, and support are often charged separately.; Custom systems are treated differently from basic websites: buyers will tolerate higher prices if the tool clearly replaces manual workflow or prevents lost revenue.; Many small businesses still use WhatsApp, Excel, paper, and Facebook pages as the operating system for bookings and enquiries because these tools are already embedded in daily work.; A low-friction offer can attract curiosity, but some enquiries will be out of scope or unready to pay later, especially where the buyer only wants a free site or a complex platform for no budget.; Trust and responsiveness matter: South African small businesses often value direct access to the founder, quick replies, and evidence the site actually works on mobile and generates leads.

Typical price range: For comparable South African offerings, the gathered evidence points to roughly R5,590–R16,900 once-off for a simple business website, with maintenance commonly around R299–R1,500 per month and some providers quoting up to R5,000+ per month for more active support. Custom web applications such as booking systems are reported at roughly R27,980+ to R60,000+ and can rise well beyond that depending on scope; those are advertised or reported signals, not confirmed market norms.
